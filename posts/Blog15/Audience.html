Experimenting on LLM can shed light on human communication strategies and give us practical implications. By comparing how an LLM explains “AIGC” (Artificial Intelligence Generated Content) to two radically different demographics—like a 80-year-old grandmother and a 10-year-old child, we can uncover the sophisticated communicative strategies it uses to bridge the gap between technical complexity and human understanding. Here is a look at the specific strategies LLMs use to tailor their message, and where they sometimes miss the mark.

1. Schema Matching: The Worldview Swap

The model understands that a “good” explanation relies on mapping new concepts to the user’s lived experience. It not only changes the word but also swaps the logic.

For the Elderly, the model frames AI as an “Apprentice.” It anchors the technology in the Humanities, referencing Van Gogh or Monet. It describes training not as data processing, but as a young student visiting a museum. It appeals to a worldview that values craftsmanship, history, and utility.

For the Child: The model pivots to ”Mega-Homework“ and ”Magic.“ It uses the relatable drudgery of homework to explain training data, and the joy of instant creation for the output. It trades utility for absurdity (e.g., “a dinosaur on Mars”), knowing that for a child, technology is a toy before it is a tool.

2. Anticipatory Pragmatics: Answering the Silent Anxiety

The most sophisticated feat is the model’s ability to anticipate the specific social anxieties of each group—answering the questions they didn’t ask.

The Child’s Anxiety: “Is it Cheating?” The model recognizes the user is likely a student subject to academic rules. It proactively frames AI as a “calculator for creativity,” offering a lesson in Digital Citizenship: You are the director, the AI is just the tool. It pre-bunks the guilt of “faking it.”

The Senior’s Anxiety: “Is it Useless?” The model anticipates pragmatic skepticism. It immediately offers domestic examples (writing recipes, fixing emails) to prove value. It answers the implicit question: “Why should I care about this at my age?”

3. The Critical Flaw: Connection Over Caution

While this tailoring creates immediate connection, it reveals a bias in the model’s training – prioritizing empathy over accuracy.

The “Robot Friend” Trap: To engage the child, the model adopts a “Best Friend” persona. This is pedagogically dangerous; it obscures the fact that AI is a corporate product, encouraging children to form parasocial relationships with a database.

The Hallucination Omission: In its effort to be comforting (to the senior) and fun (to the child), the model hides the fact that it lies. It calls itself an “encyclopedia” or “super-brain,” failing to warn either user that it often fabricates information.

4. Practical Implications for Designers & Educators

If we want to teach AI literacy effectively, we should steal the LLM’s strategies—but fix its ethical blind spots.

  • Create a “Metaphor Menu”: Stop using one-size-fits-all explanations. If you are teaching digital natives, use metaphors of gaming and scaffolding. If you are teaching digital immigrants, use metaphors of labor and tools.

  • Pre-bunk the Fear: Don’t just explain features; identify the emotional barrier. Address “academic guilt” for students and “technological inadequacy” for seniors before you even open the software.

  • Re-label the “Friend”: We must actively fight the “Robot Friend” narrative the LLM pushes on children. Replace it with “Co-pilot” or “Engine.” “Friend” implies trust; “Engine” implies utility and the need for a human driver. This subtle shift prevents emotional dependency on a probabilistic tool.
